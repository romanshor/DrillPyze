Hydraulics: steady losses and drift-flux transients

DrillPyze.hydraulics computes standpipe pressure (SPP), bottomhole pressure (BHP) and ECD for the circulating system.

  • Steady state. Pressure losses follow the borehole-hydraulics chapter of Shor, Introduction to Drilling Engineering, ch. 7 (online).

  • Transients. Pump start and stop, water hammer, surge and swab come from the single-phase limit of the drift-flux model of Wang, Khaled, Luu, Ashok & van Oort (2023, SPE-214836-MS). That model builds on Evje & Fjelde (2002). The transient model reduces exactly to the steady model when nothing changes (§5).

All quantities are SI. Pressures are gauge.

1. Rheology and steady pressure losses (chapter 7)

Rheology. The mud is Bingham plastic, \(\tau = \text{PV}\,\dot\gamma + \tau_y\), when a yield point is given. Otherwise it is Newtonian, \(\tau = \mu\dot\gamma\) (\(\tau_y = 0\), \(\text{PV} = \mu\)).

Velocity and flow regime. The mean velocity is \(v = Q/A\). With \(D\) the pipe inner diameter and \(h = D_2 - D_1\) (hole or casing ID minus pipe OD), the apparent viscosity and Reynolds number are

apparent viscosity

Reynolds number

pipe

\(\mu_a = \text{PV} + \dfrac{\tau_y D}{6v}\)

\(\text{Re} = \dfrac{\rho v D}{\mu_a}\)

annulus

\(\mu_a = \text{PV} + \dfrac{\tau_y h}{8v}\)

\(\text{Re} = \dfrac{\rho v\,(0.816\,h)}{\mu_a}\)

Flow is laminar for \(\text{Re} < 2100\). Above that it is treated as turbulent; the chapter treats the transition range the same way.

Laminar losses. The annulus is treated as a slot of gap \(h/2\):

\[\left(\frac{dp}{dL}\right)_\text{pipe} = \frac{32\,\text{PV}\,v}{D^2} + \frac{16\,\tau_y}{3D}, \qquad \left(\frac{dp}{dL}\right)_\text{ann} = \frac{48\,\text{PV}\,v}{h^2} + \frac{6\,\tau_y}{h}. \tag{1.1}\]

Turbulent losses. Fanning friction with the Blasius factor, using PV and the equivalent diameter \(D_e = D\) (pipe) or \(0.816\,h\) (annulus):

\[f = 0.0791\,\text{Re}_\text{PV}^{-1/4},\quad \text{Re}_\text{PV} = \frac{\rho v D_e}{\text{PV}}, \qquad \frac{dp}{dL} = \frac{2 f \rho v^2}{D_e}. \tag{1.2}\]

System totals. The bit nozzle loss, standpipe pressure, bottomhole pressure and ECD are

\[\Delta p_\text{bit} = \frac{\rho Q^2}{2 C_d^2 A_n^2}, \qquad \text{SPP} = \Delta p_\text{surf} + \Delta p_\text{string} + \Delta p_\text{bit} + \Delta p_\text{ann},\]
\[\text{BHP} = \rho g\,\text{TVD} + \Delta p_\text{ann}, \qquad \text{ECD} = \rho + \frac{\Delta p_\text{ann}}{g\,\text{TVD}}. \tag{1.3}\]

Here \(A_n\) is the total nozzle area (\(\pi(n/64)^2\) in² per \(n/32\) in nozzle) and \(C_d = 0.95\). The chapter gives no correlation for the surface loss, so its empirical form \(\Delta p_\text{surf} = c\,Q^m\) is used; \(c = 0\) by default.

These are the chapter’s field-unit equations written in SI. For example, its \(\mu v/(1000\,h^2)\) psi/ft (cP, ft/s, in) is \(48\,\mu v/h^2\) Pa/m. They are coded in hydraulics._coeffs and hydraulics.steady_state.

2. Transient model: single-phase drift flux

Wang et al. (2023) write mass, mixture momentum and energy equations for each flow path (drillstring bore, flowing down; annulus, flowing up) on a mesh whose cell edges are fixed in measured depth. Each cell’s cross section is volume-averaged over the string currently in it (their Fig. 1). With no gas, the drift relation drops out and the system becomes

\[\frac{\partial (\rho A)}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (\rho A u)}{\partial x} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial (\rho A u)}{\partial t} + A\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} = A\left(\rho g\cos\theta - \frac{dp}{dL}\Big|_\text{fric}\right), \tag{2.1}\]

with the liquid equation of state of their eq. 2:

\[\rho(T, p) = \bigl(1 - (T - T_\text{ref})\,c_\text{exp}\bigr)\,\rho_\text{ref} + \frac{p}{c^2}. \tag{2.2}\]

  • \(x\) is measured depth and \(u\) is positive down.

  • The convective flux \(\rho A u^2\) is dropped. The Mach number \(u/c\) is about \(10^{-3}\).

  • The speed of sound \(c\) is the effective wave speed in the conduit, Fluid.sound_speed (default 1500 m/s).

  • \(T\) comes from the wellbore thermal model when it is on (§4).

Boundary conditions (Wang et al.):

  • a prescribed volume rate at the drillstring inlet (the pump);

  • a constant pressure (back_pressure, 0 = open flowline) at the annulus outlet.

Bit. The bit nozzles are a face joining the bottom drillstring cell to the annulus cell that contains the bit. Below the bit, the rat hole is part of the annulus path and is closed at the hole bottom.

2.1 Discretisation

The scheme is staggered: mass \(m_j\) (kg) in each cell, and mass flux \(G_k\) (kg/s, positive down) on each face. A face between cells \(u\) (above) and \(d\) (below) obeys a lumped momentum balance:

\[I_k\,\frac{dG_k}{dt} = \underbrace{p_u - p_d + \bar\rho_k\,g\,(z_d - z_u)}_{\text{drive}} - \Delta p_{\text{fric},k}(G_k), \qquad I_k = \tfrac12\Bigl(\frac{L_u}{A_u} + \frac{L_d}{A_d}\Bigr),\]
\[\frac{dm_j}{dt} = G_\text{in} - G_\text{out}, \qquad p_j = c^2\Bigl(\frac{m_j}{V_j} - \rho_{T,j}\Bigr). \tag{2.3}\]

\(\Delta p_{\text{fric},k}\) is the friction of the two half cells beside the face, from §1 and evaluated at their own velocities, plus the nozzle loss on the bit face. It is written as

\[\Delta p_\text{fric} = R\,(G - G_s) + B\,\operatorname{sign}(G - G_s),\]

where \(R\) collects the viscous and turbulent parts (\(a\) in \(dp/dL = a u + b\)) and \(B\) the Bingham yield term (\(b\)). \(G_s\) is the mass flux the moving pipe drags along (§2.2).

Time step. Each step has two parts:

  1. Pressures are explicit, with an acoustic Courant limit \(c\,\Delta t \le 0.8\,L_\text{min}\).

  2. Face fluxes are backward Euler, with two Picard iterations on \(R\):

    \[G^{n+1} = \frac{G^n + \frac{\Delta t}{I}\bigl(\text{drive} + R\,G_s - B s\bigr)}{1 + \frac{\Delta t}{I}R}, \qquad G^{n+1} = G_s \ \text{ if } \ \Bigl|G^n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{I}(\text{drive} + R G_s) - (1 + \tfrac{\Delta t}{I} R)\,G_s\Bigr| \le \tfrac{\Delta t}{I} B .\]

The second line treats the yield stress as a Coulomb-like inclusion: a gelled column holds until the pressure difference breaks it. That reproduces the pressure needed to break circulation.

Discrete steady state. WellboreHydraulics.set_steady(Q) gives the exact discrete steady state. Every face carries the same mass flux, and drive = friction on every face. It is solved by marching from the outlet down the annulus, across the bit, then up the drillstring, with a fixed point on the face densities. With \(Q = 0\) it gives the hydrostatic column, which is an exact equilibrium of the scheme (well balanced).

2.2 Moving geometry: surge, swab, growth

String motion. Each ~1 s tick, the string’s position gives new volume-averaged sections for every cell. Two things follow:

  • Displacement. Within the tick, cell volumes change linearly in time, so the steel moving in displaces mud through (2.3). This is surge when running in and swab when pulling out.

  • Wall drag. Friction acts on the velocity relative to the walls.

    • In the bore, the whole wall moves with the pipe: \(u - v_p\).

    • In the annulus, a clinging constant \(K_c\) (Burkhardt 1961; default 0.45) sets the effective pipe-dragged velocity: \(u - K_c v_p\).

    • This sets \(G_s = K\,v_p\,\bar\rho A\).

Growing hole. The bottom annulus cell lengthens as the hole deepens, and the bottom drillstring cell follows the bit. Each is cut in two at 1.5 cell lengths, and the bottom drillstring cell is merged with the one above below 0.5 cell lengths. This is the grow-cut-grow scheme of Wang et al., Fig. 2: mass is split by volume and pressure is unchanged.

3. Outputs

SPP

top-cell pressure, minus the hydrostatic head from the surface to its centre, plus half-cell friction and the surface loss

BHP

bottom annulus cell pressure plus the head to the hole bottom

BHP / (g · TVD)

\(Q_\text{in}\), \(Q_\text{out}\)

pump rate, and return rate at the flowline

\(Q_\text{bit}\), \(\Delta p_\text{bit}\)

nozzle flow and nozzle loss

Simulation.hydraulics_results() returns these channels once per slow tick (~1 s). Simulation.latest() carries the latest SPP, BHP, ECD and return flow.

4. Coupling to the simulation

  • Advance. Hydraulics advances at every slow tick, which is at fixed step counts, so pausing and resuming stays exact.

  • Inputs. It uses the ramped pump rate (§6), the bit and hole depths, and the average string velocity over the tick.

  • Temperature. When the thermal model is on, its bore and annulus temperatures set \(\rho_T\) in (2.2). Heating or cooling the mud therefore changes the pressures.

  • Rock strength. The circulating BHP from hydraulics is the \(p_\text{bh}\) used by the rock-strength model (confinement P1). The thermal model’s own simplified pressure is no longer used for that.

  • Cost. Hydraulics costs about 20% of run time: Well A runs at 83× real time with it and 103× without.

6. Set-point ramps

Inputs that have no controller approach a new value linearly over a short ramp time (ModelParameters.ramps); 0 means instant. Set points that feed a controller are not ramped, because the controller shapes the response.

Kernel inputs are evaluated at every time step inside the kernel, from a table of (start value, start time, ramp time, target). Pump rate and inlet temperature are evaluated at each slow tick and inside the hydraulic substeps. When an input takes over from another mode, for example omega_td after omega_sp, its ramp starts from the present physical value, which makes the transfer bumpless.

7. Limitations

  • Single phase. Gas kicks, cuttings loading and hole cleaning are not modelled, although the drift-flux structure allows a second phase.

  • Rheology. Newtonian or Bingham only, as in chapter 7.

    • Power-law and Herschel–Bulkley losses are not included.

    • The regime switch at Re = 2100 is sharp.

    • Friction is quasi-steady: the steady correlations are used at each instant.

    • Eccentricity and tool-joint losses are ignored.

  • Surge and swab. The averaged string velocity over a ~1 s tick drives them. Axial vibrations faster than about 0.5 Hz do not pump mud in this model.

  • Yield stress. The yield point stands in for gel strength: the pressure to break circulation uses YP, not a time-dependent gel strength.

  • Wave speed and dissipation. The wave speed is one effective value, ignoring pipe-wall elasticity (Korteweg) and any difference between bore and annulus. The staggered acoustic scheme has little numerical dissipation, so acoustic ripples decay through friction alone (§5.2 figures).

  • Coupling direction. Hydraulic pressure does not act back on the string mechanics: there is no pump-open force and no pressure-area effect on WOB.
